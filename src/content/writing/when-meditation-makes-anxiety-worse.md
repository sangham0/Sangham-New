There is a particular kind of confusion that arrives when you have been meditating sincerely for some time and notice that the practice seems to be making things worse. The anxiety that meditation was supposed to quiet is louder. Sits end in restlessness or dread rather than ease. Something that is widely described as medicine seems to be acting more like an irritant.

The first response is usually self-blame. You are doing it wrong. You are not dedicated enough, or you are too tense, or your mind is simply not suited for this. The second response, sometimes, is to push harder, to apply more effort in the hope that the difficulty will break through into something better.

Neither of these responses is particularly useful, and the self-blame especially is worth setting aside early. What is happening is better understood than blamed, and it is more common than most corners of the meditation world are willing to acknowledge.

## Why Turning Inward Can Amplify Anxiety

One of the first things a sitting practice does is focus attention on inner experience. This seems straightforwardly positive, and often it is. But increased sensitivity to inner experience is a double-edged quality. The same attention that can register subtlety and stillness will also register what is already there: the low hum of background tension, the breath that is already slightly constricted, the heart rate that is already elevated.

For someone carrying a moderate or high level of background anxiety, turning inward does not necessarily calm things. It can make the anxiety more legible. That is not nothing, but it is not the same as relief.

There is also the question of arousal. Concentration itself is a form of activation. Gathering attention, holding a focus, returning again and again to an object of awareness, these require a certain energy that is closer to alertness than to rest. This is useful when the nervous system is genuinely settled. When it is already keyed up, the effort of concentration can tip the balance further in the wrong direction.

## Stillness Removes the Regulating Functions of Activity

This is one that does not get spoken about enough. A great deal of ordinary daily activity, movement, conversation, small tasks, familiar routines, serves a regulatory function. It keeps difficult material at a manageable distance. It is not avoidance in any pathological sense; it is simply how most people maintain equilibrium in ordinary life.

Sitting practice removes most of those functions at once. The body is still. There is no conversation, no task, no forward movement. What was held at a comfortable distance, through no particular fault of yours, now has space to surface.

For many practitioners, this is eventually the point. The practice does, over time, build a container spacious enough to hold what surfaces. But that capacity is not given at the start, and it does not arrive in a straight line. There are periods when what surfaces exceeds what the current practice can comfortably hold. This is a mismatch between the container and the material, not a problem with the practitioner.

## Breathing Techniques and Physiological Amplification

If your practice includes any manipulation of the breath, this deserves particular attention. Practices that extend the exhale, hold the breath, or significantly change the rhythm of breathing alter the chemistry of the body in measurable ways. Carbon dioxide levels shift. The relationship between the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the nervous system is affected. These changes can be useful and can also, depending on the person's baseline, produce sensations that are difficult to distinguish from the early stages of a panic response.

Tingling, lightheadedness, a sense of unreality, a tightening in the chest, an urgency to breathe more, these can arise from breathing techniques that are theoretically calming and still feel alarming to a nervous system already running close to its edge. The body does not always parse the difference between a deliberate practice-related sensation and a signal that something is wrong.

If this is your experience, [When Breathwork Goes Wrong](/wisdom/when-breathwork-goes-wrong) looks at this territory in more detail, particularly the ways that breath manipulation can destabilise an already sensitised system.

## The Monitoring Problem

There is one more mechanism worth naming, because it is so easy to fall into and so rarely identified as the difficulty it is. It is the problem of self-monitoring.

When anxiety is present in practice, the natural response is to watch more carefully. Am I calming down yet? Is this working? Is this the kind of experience I should be having? Each of these questions introduces a layer of evaluation, and evaluation introduces a quality of tension that is directly opposed to whatever quality of ease the practice is aimed at.

Practice becomes performance. There is now a judge in the room assessing the meditator's progress, and both the judge and the meditator are you. This recursive self-scrutiny is not a defect of character; it is an entirely predictable response to doing something that matters to you when it seems to be going wrong. But it does need to be noticed, because it can sustain and amplify anxiety as effectively as any other mechanism.

## What to Adjust Before Giving Up

None of what is described above means that practice itself is wrong for you, or that you need to stop. It may mean that the particular form of practice, or the way you are approaching it, needs some modification.

Shortening sits is one of the most straightforward adjustments. A twenty-minute sit that ends in distress does not build capacity; it reinforces the association between sitting and difficulty. Ten minutes that remain within the window of what is manageable is more useful as a starting point, and can be extended gradually as stability builds.

Keeping the eyes open or half-open changes the quality of the experience significantly. Eyes closed can intensify the inward turn and increase the likelihood that difficult material will surface without any external anchor. Eyes open or gently lowered maintains some contact with the outer world and tends to be gentler on a sensitised system.

Shifting attention from internal sensations to outer sensory experience, the sounds in the room, the feeling of the ground or chair beneath you, the quality of light, provides an anchor that is less likely to feed into the anxiety loop. Internal body sensation is where the anxiety lives; anchoring attention outside or at the boundary of the body can provide enough distance to make sitting workable.

Ordinary breathing, without any modification, is almost always preferable to extended or manipulated breath practice when the system is raw. The breath as it is, observed without interference, is sufficient as an object of attention. The instruction in [How to Sit](/wisdom/how-to-sit) is written with this kind of simplicity in mind.

Some practitioners also find it useful to introduce a period of movement, walking, gentle stretching or physical work, before sitting. Movement discharges some of the excess activation that makes stillness difficult, and arriving at the sit from a more regulated state changes the quality of what follows.

## When Adjustment Is Not Enough

There are experiences that signal something more than a technique adjustment is needed.

Panic during or after meditation: a full-scale panic response, rather than simple discomfort, is worth taking seriously. So is a pattern of dissociation, a sense of unreality or detachment that persists after practice ends, or sleep that becomes reliably disturbed following meditation. These are signs that the system is being pushed beyond its current capacity to recover.

Material that surfaces and does not settle is another category. If sitting practice is consistently bringing up content, memories, states, or feelings, that feel larger than the practice can hold, and that do not resolve within a reasonable period after the sit, that is not a sign to sit longer or more intensely. It is a signal that the container needs to be larger, which in most cases means working with a person rather than a method.

If there is any acute crisis, any thought of self-harm or harm to others, or if daily functioning is collapsing, the right support is clinical: a psychologist, GP, or psychiatrist as the first point of contact. This is not a failure of practice. It is a recognition of what each kind of support can and cannot hold.

## The Gap Between Two Worlds

One of the more difficult aspects of practice-related psychological difficulty is that the people best placed to help are rarely the same people. A psychologist or therapist may have little understanding of what meditation actually does, and may not be equipped to work with practice-related experiences without pathologising the practice itself. A meditation teacher may have deep knowledge of the territory but no psychological training, and may interpret what is arising through a contemplative lens that misses the psychological dimension.

People in this gap often stay stuck there, because neither conversation quite fits. The teacher says: go deeper, sit through it. The therapist says: perhaps meditation is not right for you. Neither response quite meets what is actually happening.

Working with someone who has trained in both areas is not a luxury for a small number of practitioners. For the people who land in this territory, it is often the thing that finally makes sense of what has been happening and allows practice to resume on a stable footing.
