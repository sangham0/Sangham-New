Mentoring for a teenage boy only works if he can speak honestly. If every uncertainty, mistake, fear, or frustration is automatically reported to a parent, the relationship becomes another place in which he has to manage how he appears.

At the same time, a parent is not a distant observer. They are responsible for their child's safety, they often initiate the enquiry, and they need to understand the boundaries of the process.

Good confidentiality holds both truths. It gives the young person a real private space while making the limits of that privacy clear from the beginning.

## Who the Service Is For

The client is the teenage boy, normally aged 12 to 17. The landing page contains information for parents because parents are often involved in choosing, arranging, and paying for the service. That does not make the parent the person receiving counselling or mentoring.

This distinction matters. The work is not a channel through which a parent sends instructions to a teenager. It is a relationship in which the young person can examine what is happening in his life, develop language for it, and take greater responsibility for how he responds.

## What Usually Stays Private

The content of ordinary sessions stays private. This can include feelings about family, school, friendship, identity, relationships, anger, uncertainty, mistakes, and the parts of himself he is still trying to understand.

Privacy does not mean secrecy for its own sake. It creates the conditions in which a teenager can move beyond automatic answers and speak from what is actually happening. Without that, mentoring becomes superficial very quickly.

I may encourage a young person to speak with a parent about something important. Where useful, we can think together about how to have that conversation. The aim is not to replace family communication but to help make more honest communication possible.

## The Limits of Confidentiality

Confidentiality is not absolute. Information may need to be shared when there is a serious concern about:

- immediate or significant risk of harm to the young person;
- a credible risk that someone else may be harmed;
- abuse, neglect, exploitation, or an unsafe environment;
- another legal or professional safeguarding duty.

These limits are explained before the work begins. Where it is safe and appropriate, I would tell the young person what needs to be shared and involve him in how that conversation happens. The purpose is protection, not punishment.

If there is an acute mental health crisis, this mentoring relationship is not a substitute for emergency, medical, psychiatric, or psychological care. The appropriate clinical or safeguarding route should be used.

## What Parents Can Expect

Parents should not expect a report of what was said in each session. They can expect clarity about:

- the purpose and scope of the work;
- scheduling, attendance, and practical arrangements;
- the general process for reviews and parent check-ins;
- whether the work remains within the scope of mentoring and wellness counselling;
- any safeguarding concern that requires action.

General progress can sometimes be discussed without disclosing private content. For example, we may speak about whether the young person is engaging, whether the original goals remain relevant, and what kind of support at home would be helpful.

The exact shape of parent involvement depends on age, maturity, circumstances, and what is agreed at the start. It should never be ambiguous.

## The First Parent Conversation

The initial parent conversation is a place to understand the concern, explain the scope and boundaries, and establish whether this service is appropriate. It is also a chance to notice whether the hoped-for outcome belongs to the young person, the parent, or both.

A parent may want a boy to become more disciplined, communicative, confident, or motivated. These are understandable wishes. But sustainable work cannot be built only around making a teenager easier to manage. He needs some ownership of why he is there and what he wants to understand or change.

## The First Conversation With the Young Person

The young person should hear the confidentiality agreement directly, in plain language. He should know what stays between us, what could not stay private, how parents may be involved, and what he can do if the relationship does not feel right.

This is part of building trust. A teenager should not have to discover the boundaries only after saying something important.

## Privacy Is a Practice, Not a Promise in Small Print

Confidentiality is more than a clause on a form. It is an ongoing practice of judgement, transparency, secure handling of personal information, and respect for the young person's developing autonomy.

It also requires humility. When a situation falls outside the scope of the work, the responsible response is to say so and help the family find more appropriate support.
